# Doomscrolling & attention — what the science actually says

The evidence-based version of "brain rot," from science journalist Joss Fong
([[doomscrolling-attention-science-bbc]]). Cuts against both the moral panic *and* the dismissiveness.

## The three findings that matter
1. **Most evidence is correlational** (heavy users report worse focus) and could be **reverse causation** —
   people who already can't focus can't look away. So the honest baseline is: *we don't have much causal
   evidence yet.*
2. **The few experimental studies** (test → 10–30 min of scrolling → re-test) do show **short-form video
   feeds degrade specific skills even after brief exposure** — chiefly **critical pausing** (reflecting on
   what you're seeing) and **prospective memory** (remembering to do an intended thing). Small; need
   replication.
3. **The mechanism is the interaction, not the content.** A passive **montage of the same clips** doesn't
   produce the deficit — it's the **swipe-to-decide** interface (rapid keep/skip judgments) that flips the
   brain into a **less reflective, more compulsive, faster** mode. The addictive design *is* the harm
   vector.

**Reversibility:** no evidence it's permanent. Fong's reframe — *"attention spans aren't broken; we're
just carrying the monkey around."* The phone is a monkey playing a trumpet next to you; **move the monkey
away for three days** and the ability to read usually returns.

## The tactic that follows (it's [[environment-design]], not willpower)
- **Time limits fail** ("only 15 minutes" never holds).
- **Context limits work:** bind scrolling to a place/activity ("only on the treadmill / while walking"),
  and don't open it otherwise. Same logic as laying out running shoes the night before — shape the
  environment so the default changes. Reconciles with the vault's **[[reticular-activating-system|RAS]]**
  (the swipe hijacks the attention filter) and **[[conscious-vs-subconscious]]** (it drops you into fast,
  subconscious mode).
