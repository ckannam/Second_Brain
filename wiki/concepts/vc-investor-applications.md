This summer at Johns Hopkins Technology Ventures I was on the capital strategy team, where evaluating a company meant more than liking the idea. We tested the strength of the science and the team behind it, benchmarked each company against comparable early financings in PitchBook to judge whether the round and valuation were realistic, and mapped every company to the specific investors most likely to lead it. That work connected me to some of the best and most hungry scientists in the world and taught me how to professionally evaluate an early team before deploying capital.

At Duke I run University Shipping, a well known and very successful student run business. That makes me two things at once. I am deeply connected to the Duke network, and I am a builder who knows firsthand what it takes to build while carrying a full courseload. So I can spot talent through the investor lens I learned at JHTV and back it through the founder lens I live every day. No matter the field, most of the problems a campus founder faces I have likely faced and overcome myself, so I can offer more than a check or advice.

Here is what would make me different as a Contrary partner at Duke. Running University Shipping has earned me warm, trusted relationships with Amy Linane and Amy Wyron Robinson, the two people who effectively run Duke's Innovation and Entrepreneurship ecosystem and who screen almost every serious company on campus. Because they know me and trust me as a builder, I can source the strongest Duke founders earlier and with more signal than a partner working from the outside in. That is the deal flow I would bring to Contrary, sourced through relationships most people on campus never get access to.

## Catalyst — answers (final)

### "What have you built in the past?"
The build I am most proud of is the expansion engine for University Shipping, the student move in and move out shipping and storage business I co own and run at Duke.

At Duke we ran margins of 70 to 80%, which made me ask why this business does not already exist on every campus. My first attempt to answer that was an expansion to Richmond, and it failed. We tried to run it on our Duke email playbook with no operator on the ground, and the logistics collapsed. The lesson was clear. Expansion is not a marketing problem, it is a local ownership problem.

So at Columbia I built the fix, and it came down to a series of deliberate decisions. First, I decided to split the model in two. We kept the demand engine centralized, because our Duke email playbook already worked, and we decentralized the physical operations to people on the ground. Second, I recruited two local operators who were already embedded in the Columbia network, and I gave them ownership of the physical move out work and the liability that came with it. Third, rather than paying them a flat wage, I restructured the incentives through a holding company so their upside was tied directly to execution. Fourth, after the Richmond loss, I chose to start lean from a standing start instead of spending big up front, so our downside stayed capped while we proved the model.

Those decisions worked. We went from nothing to over ten thousand dollars in revenue at low 70% margins in a matter of months, and we expect to more than double that this year.

What I really built was proof that the model travels. Duke was not magic, the system was. That playbook is now how we are entering five new campuses this spring.

### "What are you interested in?" (slacklining + seeking hard things)
My niche obsession is slacklining. There is nothing else like standing on a piece of one inch webbing a few feet off the ground. The moment your focus drifts, you fall, so it forces you into the present in a way almost nothing else can. Every thought about my classes, my company, or my phone disappears, because the line does not let you think about anything except the next step.

What I love is that it externalizes focus. Being fully present is something everyone knows is valuable but almost no one can train directly. Slacklining makes it physical, and the feedback could not be clearer. Lose focus and you hit the ground. That same brutal simplicity is why it resets me. An hour outside on the line pulls me out of everything else and leaves me calmer, quieter, and sharper, because for that hour the only thing that exists is the next step. It trains the exact skill that makes me better at everything else I do.

Slacklining is really just one version of something bigger about me. I am drawn to hard things on purpose. College, to me, is a time to get good at doing hard things. Not to learn exactly what one job will need from you, but to learn how to learn, and how to pick up and implement new skills the way any real job will demand.

What I think is unique about me is that I spent my college years deliberately making things harder for myself while I searched for what I wanted to do, so that when I finally found it, I would be ready to attack it. The clearest example is that I completed every pre med requirement, even though I knew it was unlikely I would ever go to medical school. I did it partly because I was genuinely fascinated by most of the coursework, but mostly because it was hard. I believed that whenever I found the thing I wanted, the harder the things I had already done, the better I would perform at it.

Every challenge, on the line or off it, comes down to the same thing. For over three years I have been thrown new problems constantly, in my coursework and in running my company, usually with no one to ask and no choice but to find the answer myself. I have done exactly that, again and again. Any skill I do not have today, I am confident I can gain and put to use quickly, because that is precisely what I have spent the last three years training for.
