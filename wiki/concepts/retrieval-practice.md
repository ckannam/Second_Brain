# Retrieval practice (the testing effect)

Actively **retrieving** information from memory (testing yourself) strengthens retention more
than **restudying** it (re-reading, re-highlighting). Distinct from the [[spacing-effect]] (which
is about *when* you practice); the two combine into [[spaced-repetition]].

## The evidence — strong in general, shaky in math
- **In general:** medium-to-large — g ≈ 0.50–0.61 across meta-analyses (Rowland 2014; Adesope
  et al. 2017; Yang et al. 2021). Retrieval also enables *transfer* to new formats/problem types
  (Pan & Rickard 2018).
- **In mathematics specifically:** the effect was **not robust** — g = 0.18 with a 95% CI that
  **crosses zero** (7 studies, 32 effect sizes). From [[spacing-math-meta-analysis-murray-2025]].
  A key reason: it isn't obvious what "restudy" even means for a math problem, and students can
  fall back on notes — so pure retrieval is hard to isolate. What *does* reliably help math is
  **spaced, [[interleaving|interleaved]] problem practice**, not flashcard-style retrieval.
- **For complex skills:** combined spacing + testing preserved clinical-skill retention at 3
  months ([[spacing-testing-complex-skills-study]]).

## The design implication (this is the important part)
Retrieval is not one thing — the *right kind of retrieval depends on the [[knowledge-types|type of
knowledge]]*:
- **Fact** → flashcard retrieval (what Anki / [[spaced-repetition]] does well).
- **Concept** → retrieval = **explain it from scratch** without looking (elaborative retrieval),
  not recognition.
- **Skill** → retrieval = **redo the problem / perform the procedure**, spaced and interleaved —
  which is what the math evidence actually supports.

A tool that schedules *one* retrieval modality (flashcards) for everything misses this. Matching
modality to type is the wedge behind the [[neuro-channel|learning-app]] idea. Grounded in the
[[neuroscience-of-behavior]] hub.
