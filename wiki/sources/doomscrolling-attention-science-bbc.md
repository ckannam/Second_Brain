# Source: BBC — what the science actually says about "brain rot"

Katty Kay's **BBC** "New Normal" series talks to science journalist **Joss Fong** (ex-Vox; her channel
*HowTown*) about the actual experimental research on
short-form video and attention. The value here is that Fong separates **correlation from causation** and
lands on a mechanism that's *not* the obvious one. Feeds the [[neuroscience-of-behavior]] cluster and
Cole's own [[cole|attention-leak]] problem; concept page: [[doomscrolling-attention-science]].

## What the studies actually found
- **Little high-quality research yet** (the fears are new). Lots of *correlational* data (heavy social
  users report worse cognitive skills) — but that could be **reverse causation** (people who already
  struggle to focus can't look away).
- **The few experimental studies** randomize people, give a cognitive test, expose them to 10–30 min of
  scrolling (TikTok/Twitter/YouTube), then re-test. A couple show **short-form video feeds specifically
  degrade cognitive skills even after brief exposure** — notably **critical pausing** ("stop and be
  critical about what you're looking at") and **prospective memory** (remembering to do a thing you
  intended). Small studies; need replication.
- **The surprising mechanism: it's the *interaction*, not the *content*.** When the *same* clips are
  stitched into a passive **montage**, the deficit largely disappears. It's the **swipe-to-decide**
  interface — the rapid keep/skip judgment — that flips the brain into a **less reflective, more
  compulsive, faster-acting state.** "That design is actually key to making them work," which led Fong to
  question whether this content form should exist at all.
- **Reversibility: unknown, but probably not doom.** "Zero evidence" it's permanent; too early to say
  it's permanently damaging. Fong's reframe: **"our attention spans are not broken — we're just carrying
  the monkey around with us all the time."**

## The practical tactic (a pure [[environment-design]] move)
- **Time limits fail** ("I'll only scroll 15 min" is a terrible strategy). **Context/domain limits work:**
  bind scrolling to a *place or activity* — "I only scroll on the treadmill / while walking" — and don't
  open it otherwise. This is context-based [[environment-design]], not willpower.
- **The "monkey playing a trumpet" metaphor:** if you can't focus on your book, it's usually because the
  phone-monkey is in the room blaring the trumpet next to you. **Move the monkey away for three days** and
  see if the reading returns.
- Honest coda: even Fong, who did the research, hasn't changed her own habits much ("there's something
  irresistible") and questions whether HowTown should keep making short-form at all — a genuine creator's
  ethical bind.
