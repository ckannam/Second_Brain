## Brief Research Proposal (~540 words; limit 400–600)
**Title:** *Editing Depth and the Illusion of Explanatory Depth: How Shallow and Deep Editing Impact Metacognitive Accuracy*

The digital age has shifted people from passive readers to active editors of online knowledge—most visibly on platforms like Wikipedia, where any user can revise a living document. This raises a metacognitive question: when people edit information, how does it change what they think they know? A useful lens is the Illusion of Explanatory Depth (IoED)—people overestimate their understanding of everyday things until they try to explain them in detail (Rozenblit & Keil, 2002). Generating a full explanation normally "breaks" this illusion, producing a drop in confidence. But recent work finds that *editing* an explanation produces no net change in confidence (Park & Marsh, in prep). I propose that this null effect hides two opposing processes: shallow versus deep edits engage different levels of processing and should move metacognition in opposite directions.

This study tests whether the depth of an editing task determines the accuracy of subsequent knowledge and credit judgments. Participants are randomly assigned to one of three conditions: Explain (generate a full explanation from scratch), Shallow Edit (fix only spelling, grammar, and formatting in a provided "bare-bones" explanation), or Deep Edit (add three substantive mechanical details retrieved from memory). Bare-bones stimuli for five everyday phenomena (e.g., "Why are there leap years?") are pilot-validated to contain no more information than a participant already possesses, so any change in judgments reflects metacognition rather than newly learned content. I hypothesize that Shallow Edits maintain or inflate confidence (the illusion persists), whereas Deep Edits calibrate it—producing a confidence drop like a full explanation—because they force direct retrieval. I further predict that Shallow Editors overclaim credit, misattributing the fluency of "fixing" a text to their own authorship.

Participants rate their explanatory ability on a 1–7 scale before the task (T1), after it (T2), and again after generating a full explanation from scratch (T3), letting us test whether deep editing delivers an earlier "reality check." They then estimate the percentage of the final text produced by their own edits (credit attribution) and report subjective difficulty. The study runs on a Qualtrics interface that simulates Wikipedia, with embedded attention checks and a post-task disclosure of outside-resource use; participants who used external tools are removed from analysis. All participants give informed consent and receive an educational debriefing. The primary analyses are repeated-measures ANOVAs on the T1→T2→T3 knowledge ratings by condition, plus a between-groups comparison of credit attribution.

This project is already underway and IRB-approved: I have collected roughly 90 of a target N = 250 participants through Duke's SONA subject pool. The requested funds would compensate the remaining ~160 participants needed to reach that target within the thesis timeline. SONA course-credit recruitment alone has been slow relative to the sample size required for adequately powered three-condition comparisons; paying participants—following our lab's established Prolific protocol—lets me complete data collection this academic year rather than stretching it across multiple terms.

Understanding when editing calibrates versus inflates knowledge has direct implications for how people learn in collaborative digital environments, where "fixing" text is constant and the line between the clarity of a document and one's own understanding is easily blurred. By isolating editing depth as the driver, this study clarifies why merely interacting with information can leave people more confident than correct.
